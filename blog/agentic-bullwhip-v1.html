Supply Chain · Experiment Writeup

Agentic Bullwhip Effect — Version 1

I placed LLM agents in charge of ordering decisions across a three-tier supply chain and varied two things: how much context the agent had, and how capable the model was. All four configurations amplified demand variability. One of them produced a pattern I was not expecting and have not found described in the literature.

Date February 2026
Experiment Agentic Bullwhip Effect V1
Design 2×2 Factorial · 20 runs · 720 LLM calls

Abstract

The bullwhip effect is well-understood territory in supply chain research. Forrester identified it in 1961, Lee formalised the causes in 1997. The standard prediction: demand variability amplifies at each upstream tier.

This experiment placed LLM agents at each tier of a 3-tier serial supply chain — an Indian automotive OEM, its lighting ancillary, and a component manufacturer. A 2×2 factorial design crossed two context levels (blind vs. full business context) against two model tiers (gpt-4.1-mini vs. o1), with 5 runs per configuration and 720 total LLM calls.

I designed this as an exploratory study. The primary purpose was to establish baselines — to have a measured reference point against which I could identify what behaved as expected and what did not.

Key findings

What I found

  1. Universal amplification. Every configuration, every tier, every run — OVAR above 1.0. No agent dampened demand variability. The best chain-average was 2.929 (context_lightweight). Expected, but useful to have confirmed numerically.
  2. Context had opposite effects depending on the model. For gpt-4.1-mini, context reduced chain OVAR by 7.2%. For o1, context increased it by 15.0%. The same intervention, applied to two models, moved the metric in opposite directions. This was not something I had anticipated in the experimental design.

    It has a practical implication that is worth being direct about: the common assumption that giving a more capable model more context will produce better results did not hold here. The most capable model with the most context produced the worst result of the four conditions.
  3. The inverted cascade. This is the finding that prompted Version 2.

    Three of the four configurations followed the classical bullwhip pattern — OVAR increasing at each upstream tier. OEM lowest, component highest. The monotone cascade that appears in every supply chain textbook.

    The context_reasoning configuration reversed it. OEM OVAR: 6.349. Ancillary: 4.191. Component: 2.698.

    I set up the baselines specifically to have a reference point — to be able to see when something deviated from what theory would predict. This deviated clearly. The tier closest to the consumer was the noisiest node in the chain. The tier furthest away was the quietest.

    My read on why: the o1 model, given context about the OEM's market position and the seasonal calendar, appears to have built anticipatory ordering strategies at the demand-facing tier. It tried to get ahead of expected demand patterns. In doing so, it injected variance at the head of the chain that exceeded the amplification at the downstream tiers. The component tier, receiving an already highly variable signal, had less room to amplify further relative to its input.

    If this interpretation is correct, it means a sufficiently capable model with sufficient context does not just fail to solve the bullwhip effect — it can create variance at a point in the chain where classical theory does not expect it. That is a different kind of failure than simply amplifying too much.

    I should be clear about the limits of this finding: n = 5 per configuration, and the o1 runs showed high variability (CV of 22–57% across tiers). This is a pattern that needs replication, not a conclusion. Version 2 was designed with 20 runs per cell in part to test whether it holds up.
  4. The most expensive model performed worst. o1 costs roughly 50× more per call than gpt-4.1-mini. It produced higher chain OVAR in both blind and context conditions. This is a straightforward finding but one that matters for anyone evaluating these models for operational use.

Discussion

What this means

The interaction between context and model capability is the finding I keep returning to. It cuts against the default assumption in most applied AI work — that better models with better context will produce better outcomes.

In a supply chain, the agent's output in one period affects the system state in subsequent periods. An order placed in period 3 changes the inventory position that informs the order in period 5. When the demand-facing agent introduces anticipatory variance — ordering ahead of an expected spike — that variance propagates downstream through agents that have no visibility into why their incoming orders look the way they do.

The classical bullwhip model assumes variance is amplified at each stage. The inverted cascade suggests that when agents actively construct strategies — rather than reacting passively to incoming signals — the variance can originate at the demand-facing tier in quantities that exceed the natural amplification downstream.

Whether this is a robust phenomenon or an artefact of small sample size is what Version 2 was partly designed to investigate. As it turned out, the more fundamental result from Version 2 — that heuristics dominate outright — is the larger finding. But the cascade inversion remains an interesting question for future work with stateful agents.

Scope and limitations

Exploratory study. Five runs per configuration. The o1 means carry wide uncertainty and should be treated as directional. Single product. Stateless agents. Deterministic lead times. The context treatment provided company identity and calendar month — not demand forecasts or historical data. All scenarios, companies, and supply chain structures are fictional. No proprietary data was used.
