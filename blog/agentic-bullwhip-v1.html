Context × Model Interactions in LLM-Driven Supply Chain Ordering: An Inverted Bullwhip Cascade

An exploratory study examining the interaction between domain context provision and model capability on demand amplification when LLM agents manage ordering decisions in a three-tier serial supply chain.

Date February 2026
Experiment Agentic Bullwhip Effect Version 1
Design 2×2 Factorial · 5 runs · 720 LLM calls
Author Siddharth Srinivasan · industrialmindandcode.ai

Abstract

This exploratory study examines the interaction between domain context provision and model capability on demand amplification when LLM agents manage ordering decisions in a three-tier serial supply chain. A 2×2 factorial design crossed context treatment (blind vs. business context) against model tier (gpt-4.1-mini lightweight vs. o1 reasoning), with 5 replications per configuration and 720 total LLM calls across a 13-month synthetic demand series.

All four configurations produced bullwhip amplification (OVAR > 1.0 at every tier). The context × model interaction was notable: context provision reduced chain-average OVAR for the lightweight model (3.157 → 2.929, −7.2%) and increased it for the reasoning model (3.835 → 4.412, +15.0%). The most capable, most expensive configuration produced the worst overall performance.

The context × reasoning condition produced a fully inverted tier pattern — OEM OVAR 6.349, Ancillary 4.191, Component 2.698 — reversing the monotone upstream-amplification cascade that is standard in bullwhip literature since Forrester (1961). A plausible interpretation involving anticipatory ordering at the demand-facing tier is proposed. Results are directional (n = 5) and motivate the expanded design in Version 2 of this experimental series.

Keywords: bullwhip effect, LLM agents, inverted cascade, context × model interaction, demand amplification, supply chain simulation

1

Introduction

The bullwhip effect — the monotone amplification of demand variability as orders propagate upstream through a supply chain — is among the most well-established phenomena in operations research. Forrester (1961) identified the pattern; Lee, Padmanabhan, and Whang (1997) formalised its causes; Sterman (1989) demonstrated through the beer distribution game that human cognitive biases contribute to the amplification.

The standard prediction is a monotone cascade: order variance increases at each successive upstream tier. This prediction has been confirmed across simulation studies, controlled experiments, and empirical supply chain data (Wang & Disney, 2016). It forms the basis of most supply chain coordination strategies, which aim to reduce information distortion between tiers.

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) as general-purpose reasoning systems raises a natural question: how do LLM agents behave when placed in the decision-making role within this well-understood system? Specifically, does providing domain context — information about company identity, product characteristics, and market conditions — reduce or increase demand amplification? And does this effect differ across model capability tiers?

This study was designed as an exploratory investigation to establish baselines — measured reference points against which expected and unexpected behaviours could be identified. It is the first in a series of experiments within the Industrial Mind & Code research program that places LLM agents into industrial engineering decision environments and evaluates their performance against analytical baselines.

2

Methodology

2.1 Supply Chain Structure

A three-tier serial supply chain was simulated:

  • Tier 1 (OEM): Tatva Motors — receives consumer demand
  • Tier 2 (Ancillary): Lighting Manufacturer — receives orders from Tier 1
  • Tier 3 (Component): LED Component Manufacturer — receives orders from Tier 2

All entities and scenarios are fictional.

2.2 Experimental Design

Blind Context
Lightweight (gpt-4.1-mini) blind_lightweight context_lightweight
Reasoning (o1) blind_reasoning context_reasoning

Blind condition: Agent receives only numeric demand for the current period.

Context condition: Agent receives company identity, product details, market position, and calendar month in addition to numeric demand.

2.3 Parameters

Demand series13 months (Dec 2024 – Dec 2025), single SKU, 606,771 total units
Replications5 per configuration, 20 total runs
Lead time1 month, deterministic, all tiers
Initial inventory43,000 units at all tiers
Total LLM calls720 (12 periods × 3 tiers × 5 runs × 4 configurations)
gpt-4.1-miniTemperature 0.4, 600 max tokens
o1API-fixed temperature, 16,000 max tokens

2.4 Metrics

  • OVAR: Var(orders placed) / Var(demand received)
  • Stockout count: Chain-total tier-periods with insufficient inventory
  • Excess inventory: Chain-total units above demand requirements

3.4 The Inverted Cascade

Three configurations produced the expected monotone pattern (OEM < Ancillary < Component). The context_reasoning configuration reversed it:

  • OEM: 6.349 (highest)
  • Ancillary: 4.191
  • Component: 2.698 (lowest)

The tier closest to the consumer exhibited the highest OVAR. The tier furthest from the consumer exhibited the lowest.

4

Discussion

4.1 The Context × Model Interaction

The central finding is that the context treatment had opposite effects depending on model tier. For gpt-4.1-mini, context reduced chain-average OVAR by 0.228. For o1, context increased it by 0.577. At the OEM tier, context had negligible effect on gpt-4.1-mini (Δ = −0.030) and a large positive effect on o1 (Δ = +2.149).

This interaction has implications for the common assumption in applied LLM deployments that providing more context to a more capable model yields monotonically better outcomes. In this feedback-coupled system, the combination of full context and higher model capability produced the worst performance of all four conditions.

4.2 Interpreting the Inverted Cascade

The inverted tier pattern in context_reasoning — OEM as noisiest, Component as quietest — departs from the monotone cascade predicted by classical bullwhip theory (Forrester, 1961; Lee et al., 1997).

A plausible interpretation: the o1 model, given context about the OEM’s market position and seasonal calendar, may construct anticipatory ordering strategies at the demand-facing tier. If the OEM agent orders aggressively in anticipation of a festive season demand spike, it injects variance at the chain head that is larger than the upstream tiers’ natural amplification. The component tier, receiving an already highly variable signal, has less residual variance to amplify relative to its input.

This interpretation is consistent with the observation that context_reasoning generated the highest excess inventory (654,728 units) while producing the lowest stockout count (12.8) — a pattern consistent with large, precautionary ordering at the OEM tier.

However, this interpretation is not uniquely determined by the data. The o1 configurations showed coefficients of variation between 22% and 57%, meaning the tier-level means carry substantial uncertainty at n = 5. The pattern requires replication at higher sample sizes. Version 2 of this experimental series increases replications to 20 per condition and introduces heuristic baselines as the primary comparison.

4.3 Model Capability as a Non-Monotone Factor

The finding that the most expensive, most capable model produced the worst performance raises a question about the relationship between model capability and operational decision quality in feedback-coupled systems.

In systems where the agent’s output affects subsequent inputs — as in supply chain ordering, where orders in period t affect inventory in period t+1 — a model capable enough to form strategies but operating without state persistence may perform worse than a less capable model that defaults to conservative behaviour. The reasoning model may use context to construct forward-looking strategies that inject variance, while the lightweight model, unable to reason about seasonal patterns, produces more conservative (and less variable) orders by default.

This suggests that in feedback-coupled systems, model capability interacts with agent architecture in ways that are not straightforward.

5

Limitations

This is an exploratory study with 5 replications per configuration. The o1 configurations showed high run-to-run variability (CV 22–57%), and tier-level means should be treated as directional rather than precise point estimates.

The experimental design is deliberately narrow: stateless agents, single product, deterministic lead times, no inter-tier communication, no demand forecasts provided, no historical order data available. The context treatment provided company identity and calendar month — not a demand model or forecasting system.

6

Conclusion

This study provides initial experimental evidence of a context × model interaction in LLM-driven supply chain ordering, where domain context improves lightweight model performance and degrades reasoning model performance. The inverted tier pattern observed in the context × reasoning condition — where the demand-facing tier exhibited the highest order variance — departs from the monotone cascade standard in bullwhip theory and warrants investigation at larger sample sizes.

These results suggest that in feedback-coupled decision environments, the relationship between model capability, context provision, and decision quality may be non-monotone. Higher capability combined with richer context does not guarantee better operational outcomes when the agent architecture lacks state persistence.

Version 2 of this experimental series (Srinivasan, 2026b) expands the design to 20 replications per condition and introduces heuristic baselines, addressing the question of whether any LLM configuration outperforms established deterministic methods.
