Testing the limits of the ordering formula

Siddharth Srinivasan · April 2026

If a classifier made every decision correctly, would the ordering system finally improve? This experiment removed the language model and tested perfect labels, simple rules, and fourteen control variants. None met the predeclared gate for proceeding to another language-model phase.

Why there is no model in this experiment

The preceding architecture translated five demand-direction labels into safety-stock multipliers and fed them into an Order-Up-To formula. Its model results clustered near the formula-only baseline. Two explanations remained: the classifiers were not accurate enough, or the interface and formula limited what better classification could accomplish.

V5 tested the second possibility without paying for further model inference. An oracle supplied labels from the simulation's own ground-truth schedule. Hand-written causal rules supplied another comparison. Oracle information is an experimental control, not something a real planner could obtain in advance.

The data and method

The fictional three-tier chain used 36 months of synthetic seasonal demand, injected pandemic, conflict, and port-strike events, and stochastic lead times and fill rates. The environment followed the earlier V4 design. There were twenty runs per condition.

Fourteen variants changed the multiplier ranges, the meaning of the neutral label, order damping, forecast inputs, or label source. The baselines included exponential smoothing, demand passthrough, and the unmodified Order-Up-To policy. No language model was tested in any of these conditions.

The main measure was chain OVAR, averaging each tier's order variance divided by demand variance. Stockouts were also recorded. To justify the planned second phase, a candidate had to improve OVAR over Order-Up-To by at least 0.10 or come within 0.30 of exponential smoothing.

The best candidate removed the safety-stock addition during neutral periods. Its OVAR improvement over Order-Up-To was only about 0.019, and its gap to smoothing remained about 0.540. Both gate criteria failed. Phase 2 language-model runs were therefore not justified for this design.

Perfect labels on the original lookup produced a higher OVAR point estimate than the unmodified formula. Expanding the multiplier range also increased OVAR in the tested variants. A deterministic calendar rule achieved 1.7493—slightly below the formula-only value—but the small difference did not meet the gate.

These comparisons also expose a service trade-off. Smoothing had substantially lower OVAR, but its stockout mean was 89.5 against 87.3 for Order-Up-To. Lower variance is not synonymous with a better result on every operational measure.

What the result means

Better labels did not rescue the tested label-to-buffer architecture. That supports changing the control interface rather than simply selecting a stronger language model for it. It is not a proof about every possible lookup, ordering formula, or supply chain.

The oracle's labels are perfect according to the simulation's direction categories; they are not an oracle for the optimal replenishment action. The result is bounded to fourteen tested variants, one topology, one demand profile, and this stochastic environment. The hypotheses were documented before execution but not externally pre-registered.

Next steps

This line of intent-classification experiments closed at the gate. The next design moved the model's control to the smoothing coefficient itself, allowing the formula to determine orders without a safety-stock multiplier. That was a change in architecture, not another attempt to improve the classifier within V5.
