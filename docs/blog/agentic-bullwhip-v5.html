Testing the limits of the ordering formula

Siddharth Srinivasan · April 2026

Before testing another language model, I asked whether perfect direction labels would improve the ordering system at all. This experiment used simulation-derived labels, simple rules, and fourteen control variants—with no language-model inference. None met the gate for proceeding to another model phase.

Testing the interface without the model

The previous architecture converted five demand-direction labels into safety-stock multipliers, then passed them to an Order-Up-To formula. Its model results clustered near the formula-only baseline. Perhaps the classifiers needed to improve; perhaps the interface and formula limited what better labels could accomplish.

V5 tested that second possibility. An oracle supplied labels from the simulation's ground-truth schedule, while hand-written causal rules provided another comparison. The oracle was a control for the experiment, not information a real planner could know in advance.

Fourteen variants and a gate for continuing

The fictional three-tier chain followed the earlier V4 environment: 36 months of synthetic seasonal demand, injected pandemic, conflict, and port-strike events, and stochastic lead times and fill rates. Each condition ran twenty times.

The fourteen variants changed multiplier ranges, the meaning of neutral, order damping, forecast inputs, or the source of labels. Exponential smoothing, demand passthrough, and the unmodified Order-Up-To policy supplied baselines. None of these conditions used a language model.

I measured chain OVAR, the average of each tier's ratio of order variance to demand variance, alongside stockouts. The planned second phase had a concrete entry condition: improve OVAR over Order-Up-To by at least 0.10 or come within 0.30 of exponential smoothing.

The best candidate omitted the safety-stock addition during neutral periods. It improved OVAR over Order-Up-To by only about 0.019 and remained about 0.540 away from smoothing. Neither gate criterion was met, so Phase 2 language-model runs were not justified for this design.

Perfect labels with the original lookup produced a higher OVAR point estimate than the unmodified formula. Wider multiplier ranges also increased OVAR in the tested variants. A deterministic calendar rule reached 1.7493, slightly below the formula-only value, but that difference was too small to meet the gate.

There was still a service trade-off. Smoothing recorded substantially lower OVAR, but averaged 89.5 stockouts against 87.3 for Order-Up-To. Lower order variance did not mean a better result on every operational measure.

What “perfect labels” did not mean

The oracle was perfect according to the simulation's direction categories. It did not supply optimal replenishment actions. That distinction keeps the conclusion narrow: better labels did not rescue the tested label-to-buffer architecture, not that every ordering formula or supply chain has the same limitation.

The evidence covers fourteen variants, one topology, one demand profile, and this stochastic environment. The hypotheses were documented before execution but were not externally pre-registered.

Changing the control point

This line of intent-classification experiments stopped at the gate. The results supported testing a different control interface rather than simply choosing a stronger classifier. The next design let the model select the smoothing coefficient itself, with the formula determining orders without a safety-stock multiplier. That was a new architecture, not another model trial within V5.

Conclusion

None of the fourteen tested variants met the gate for another language-model phase. Even perfect simulation-defined direction labels did not rescue the original label-to-buffer design. The evidence supported stopping that line of experiments and testing a different control interface, not claiming that all ordering architectures face the same limit or that the oracle supplied optimal orders.
