Does context help AI place supply chain orders?

Siddharth Srinivasan · February 2026

Would an AI place steadier orders if it knew more about the business it was ordering for? In this early experiment, adding context reduced order variability for gpt-4.1-mini but increased it for o1. Neither model avoided amplification of demand fluctuations.

Giving the same numbers a business setting

The bullwhip effect describes how demand fluctuations can become larger order swings as they move through a supply chain. I wanted to know whether company, product, market, and calendar information would help a language model limit those swings.

I compared gpt-4.1-mini and o1 under two conditions. In the blind condition, the agent received numerical inventory state alone. In the context condition, it received the same numbers plus a fictional business identity and calendar information. Each agent chose orders without remembering its earlier decisions.

The simulation and the original comparison

A fictional vehicle manufacturer, lighting supplier, and component supplier formed the chain. Each tier started with 43,000 units of inventory and had a fixed one-month lead time. Every run used the same 13-month synthetic demand series, from December 2024 to December 2025, with twelve active ordering periods.

The main measure was chain OVAR: order variance divided by the variance of demand received at each tier, then averaged across the chain. Below one means damping; above one means amplification. I also recorded stockouts and excess inventory. Fewer shortages are not a free improvement if they require much more stock.

The design called for five runs per configuration, but one stored run in each lightweight condition identified a different model. Those runs are excluded from the published gpt-4.1-mini comparison, leaving four per condition. The o1 comparison retains five. Older aggregate files still describe the original five-run lightweight set; the table below uses the corrected comparison.

For gpt-4.1-mini, context reduced chain OVAR by about 7.5%. For o1, it increased OVAR by 0.577. All four means remained above one, and o1 with context had the largest mean amplification. The o1 runs also varied substantially, so these means are directional observations rather than precise estimates of operational performance.

Stockouts tell another part of the story. The o1 context condition had fewer stockouts but substantially more excess inventory: its mean chain stockout count was 12.8, and its excess-inventory measure was 654,728 units. Looking only at shortages would miss that trade-off.

A later check of the lightweight result

In July 2026, I repeated the two lightweight conditions with five runs each, using the pinned version gpt-4.1-mini-2025-04-14. Chain OVAR was 3.088 ± 0.091 blind and 2.794 ± 0.080 with context. The ± values are standard deviations across runs. The 9.5% reduction pointed in the same direction as the original lightweight result.

This later batch checks that arm only. It does not replace the original four-condition comparison, rerun o1, or provide a simultaneous comparison between model versions.

What this first experiment could—and could not—show

The study was exploratory: one demand series, one product, one simplified chain, and no deterministic ordering baseline. Order limits constrained extreme outputs. It cannot establish that context generally helps, that reasoning models generally hurt, or that a different memory design would produce the same result. The hypotheses were not pre-registered.

The next step was therefore to add simple ordering rules and more repetitions. Choosing the better of two AI configurations is a different question from asking whether either improves on a straightforward rule.

Conclusion

Business context did not help both models in this exploratory setup: it reduced mean order variance for gpt-4.1-mini and increased it for o1. All four original configurations still amplified demand variability. The later lightweight replication supported the same direction of change, but without a deterministic baseline this study cannot establish that either model improves on a simple ordering rule.
