Letting AI adjust the safety-stock buffer

Siddharth Srinivasan · April 2026

Instead of asking AI for an order quantity, I gave it a narrower job: choose the safety-stock multiplier and let a formula do the arithmetic. Every tested model condition still produced more order variance than leaving that multiplier fixed.

The question and the models

Safety stock is inventory held above a forecast to protect against shortages. This experiment asked whether language models could use seasonal information to adjust that buffer without destabilising ordering. It tested gpt-4.1-mini, o4-mini, and the local nemotron-3-super:120b model.

The model chose a multiplier between 0.5 and 3.0. That multiplier scaled the safety-stock term, not the entire order. An Order-Up-To formula combined the forecast, buffer, and current inventory position to calculate the order. This distinction matters: a 1.2 multiplier meant a 20% larger safety buffer, not necessarily a 20% larger order.

The data and method

The fictional three-tier chain used one product, fixed one-month lead times, and 25 months of synthetic demand shaped around Indian automotive seasonal patterns. There were no disruption events or stochastic supply delays.

The Azure models ran twenty repetitions per information condition; the stored Nemotron results contain ten per condition. Blind supplied numerical state only. Context added the calendar and seasonal flags. Stateful added the last three periods of demand, orders, multiplier choices, backlog, and stockouts. A parsing failure fell back to a neutral multiplier of 1.0.

Two deterministic controls made the comparison interpretable. Exponential smoothing supplied a simple forecasting reference. The hybrid control used exactly the model's ordering formula but kept the multiplier at 1.0. Comparing with that control isolates the contribution of active multiplier selection within this design.

The main measure, chain OVAR, averages each tier's ratio of order variance to demand variance. Below one means damping; above one means amplification. Stockouts and inventory were also recorded, because calmer orders are not useful if they simply leave demand unmet.

All nine model conditions exceeded the fixed-multiplier control's OVAR. They did produce fewer stockouts than that control, so the result is not that every outcome became worse. Exponential smoothing, however, achieved both lower OVAR and fewer stockouts than every model condition.

Context increased OVAR for Nemotron and gpt-4.1-mini, while slightly reducing it for o4-mini. Adding history improved Nemotron and gpt-4.1-mini relative to their context conditions, but by only 0.0783 and 0.2537 OVAR. The hypothesis required an improvement of at least 0.5 for at least two models. Those smaller improvements therefore did not pass it; o4-mini became worse with history.

All four stated hypotheses were rejected. The highest seasonal multiplier-pattern score was 0.3977, below the required 0.50. Qualitatively plausible explanations about busy seasons did not translate into the required operational performance.

Limits and next steps

The result concerns a continuous multiplier attached to this particular ordering formula. It does not establish that all model-assisted control fails, or that memory is inherently harmful. The models, one synthetic demand profile, fixed lead times, and unchanged smoothing parameter limit the conclusion. The hypotheses were not formally pre-registered.

The next design replaced the free-form multiplier with a small set of labels mapped to fixed values. That would test whether removing numerical calibration from the model's task could improve the outcome. This experiment motivated that question; it did not answer it.
