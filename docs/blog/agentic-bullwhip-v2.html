Language models against simple ordering rules

Siddharth Srinivasan · March 2026

A language model can explain an order, but can it choose a better quantity than a simple rule? In this simulated supply chain, every tested rule-based baseline achieved both lower order variance and fewer stockouts than every language-model condition.

Putting direct AI ordering against a baseline

Orders can fluctuate more than the demand they respond to, with those swings growing as they move from a manufacturer to its suppliers. This is the bullwhip effect. I tested whether language models could limit it while keeping shortages low.

The models were gpt-4.1-mini and o4-mini through Azure, and phi4:14b and GPT OSS 120B locally through Ollama. Each chose order quantities from either numerical inventory information alone or the same numbers with a tier-specific role and calendar context. Together, these formed eight model-and-information combinations.

One demand series, two kinds of comparison

The fictional chain contained a vehicle manufacturer, a lighting supplier, and a component supplier. It served one product over a 25-month synthetic demand series covering two Indian festive cycles, with fixed one-month lead times. Runs started from the same initial conditions. Each model chose orders without remembering earlier decisions.

Each model condition ran twenty times. Three deterministic policies provided reference points: exponential smoothing with α = 0.30, passing demand through directly, and ordering up to a stock target with a fixed safety buffer. Each rule ran once because its behaviour was deterministic in this fixed environment. That single result does not measure reliability across different demand scenarios.

I read chain OVAR and stockouts together. OVAR divides order variance by demand variance at each tier, then averages across the three tiers. Above one means amplification; below one means damping. The design required an OVAR difference of at least 0.5 for a practically meaningful claim. Reducing variance at the expense of service was not enough.

The model-condition means ranged from 4.33 to 6.35 OVAR. Every heuristic recorded lower OVAR and fewer stockouts than every model configuration. The models were not gaining better service in exchange for more variable orders. All seven stated hypotheses were rejected.

Additional context was not a consistent remedy

For gpt-4.1-mini, context reduced OVAR from 4.70 to 4.47, short of the practical-importance threshold. For phi4:14b, it increased OVAR from 4.33 to 6.35; the context condition had a standard deviation of 2.53. That model's ordering was much less predictable across runs with the extra information. The reasoning models also did not deliver the expected operational advantage.

Changing the job assigned to the model

This result concerns models choosing order quantities directly. It says nothing about their usefulness for reading documents, interpreting unusual signals, or advising a planner—tasks this experiment did not evaluate.

Lack of memory is a possible contributor, but memory was not independently varied. The finding remains a comparison of these agents, prompts, baselines, and one synthetic series, not a universal explanation of model behaviour.

The next line of investigation gave the model a narrower role: inform an ordering formula rather than replace it. The requirement remained the same. Adding a model needed to improve an operational outcome, not merely produce a plausible explanation.
