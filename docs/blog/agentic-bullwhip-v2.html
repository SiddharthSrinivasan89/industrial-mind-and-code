Supply Chain · Experiment Writeup

Agentic Bullwhip Effect: Version 2

Version 1 showed that all LLM configurations amplified demand variability. Version 2 asks the follow-up question: how do they compare against a heuristic? I tested four models against three deterministic baselines across 20 runs each. Every heuristic won, on both metrics, in every condition.

Date March 2026
Experiment Agentic Bullwhip Effect Version 2
Design 2×2 Factorial · 20 runs · 11,520 LLM calls

Abstract

After Version 1 established that every LLM configuration amplified the bullwhip effect, the natural next question was whether any configuration could at least match a simple heuristic.

I tested four models across two axes — frontier vs. local inference, lightweight vs. reasoning — against three deterministic baselines: exponential smoothing, naive passthrough, and order-up-to with fixed safety stock. Twenty independent runs per LLM condition, 11,520 total LLM calls, over a 25-month synthetic demand series with Indian automotive seasonality.

The heuristics were better on both order variance and stockout count in every comparison. Not a tradeoff between metrics — the LLM configurations were outperformed on both at once.

Key findings

What I found

  1. The heuristics won on both metrics simultaneously. Exponential smoothing: chain OVAR 0.54, 5 stockouts. Best LLM (phi4:14b blind): OVAR 4.33, 41 stockouts. Every LLM configuration fell in the 4.33–6.35 range on OVAR and 37–43 on stockouts. The heuristics and the LLMs occupied different regions of the performance space entirely.

    I expected the direction. A probabilistic model generating orders in a deterministic environment — fixed lead times, single product, no stochastic disruptions — does not have an advantage over a formula that was designed for exactly this class of problem. What the experiment quantified is how large the gap actually is, and that no reasonable configuration change would close it within this experimental setup.
  2. Business context helped one model marginally and destabilised another. For gpt-4.1-mini (frontier), context reduced OVAR from 4.70 to 4.47. That delta of 0.23 falls below the MPRD threshold I set at 0.5 before running the experiment. A real effect, but not one I would act on.

    For phi4:14b (local), context pushed chain OVAR from 4.33 to 6.35. More concerning was the Ancillary-tier result: OVAR of 10.82 with a standard deviation of 8.14 across 20 runs. That variance is the finding. It means the model did not get consistently worse with context — it became unpredictable. Some runs looked almost normal. Others were extreme. In a production system, that inconsistency is harder to manage than steady underperformance, because you cannot plan around behaviour you cannot forecast.

    The blind version of phi4:14b, by contrast, produced a standard deviation of 0.00 on chain OVAR. Every run was identical. It failed, but it failed the same way every time.
  3. Reasoning tokens did not translate into better orders. o4-mini generated over 1,000,000 reasoning tokens across 20 runs. Its blind OVAR was 4.72. gpt-4.1-mini's was 4.70. The 116-billion-parameter gpt-oss:120b running locally came in at 4.52. All statistically indistinguishable. More parameters and more computation produced the same ordering quality.

    All seven hypotheses I pre-registered were rejected.

Discussion

Why the formula won

The explanation is structural. Each LLM agent is stateless — it sees the current period's demand and places an order. It has no memory of what it ordered last period, no visibility into whether that order caused excess inventory or a stockout. It cannot self-correct because it does not know it made an error.

Exponential smoothing carries one number forward — the smoothed demand estimate. That single state variable gives it something the LLM agent does not have: continuity. An over-forecast in period 6 is partially corrected in period 7. The formula learns, in a limited but real sense, from its own history. The LLM agent, by design, does not.

This is not a limitation of the models' intelligence. It is a consequence of the agent architecture. A stateless agent in a sequential decision environment is at a fundamental disadvantage against any method that maintains state, however simple that state may be.

The phi4:14b context result warrants specific discussion. The standard deviation of 8.14 on Ancillary-tier OVAR across 20 runs indicates that the business context prompt did not consistently shape behaviour in one direction — it introduced a source of variance. In certain runs, the context may have triggered aggressive anticipatory ordering; in others, conservative behaviour. The blind model, by contrast, produced essentially identical orders across all 20 runs (standard deviation of 0.00 on chain OVAR), which means it failed predictably and consistently. From a deployment standpoint, consistent underperformance is easier to diagnose and mitigate than intermittent instability.

Where LLMs might add value in this kind of problem is not in placing orders but in reading conditions — detecting a demand regime change, flagging an anomaly in the incoming signal, recommending an adjustment to the smoothing parameter. The LLM interprets; the formula executes. That architecture would play to the strengths of each. I have not tested it. It is a hypothesis that comes out of these results, and a possible direction for a future experiment.

Scope and limitations

These results apply to a specific setup: stateless agents, single product, deterministic lead times, no inter-tier communication, no memory between periods. The demand series has seasonality but no structural breaks or supply disruptions. The conclusion I draw: LLM agents do not outperform deterministic heuristics in stylised single-product replenishment with fixed lead times and stateless execution. Whether adding memory, multi-product complexity, stochastic lead times, or unstructured context changes this result is an open question and a natural direction for future work. All scenarios, companies, and supply chain structures are fictional. No proprietary data was used.
