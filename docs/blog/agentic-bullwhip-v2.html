Language models against simple ordering rules

Siddharth Srinivasan · March 2026

Can a language model place replenishment orders more effectively than a simple rule? In this simulated supply chain, every tested rule-based baseline produced both lower order variance and fewer stockouts than every language-model condition.

The question and the models

The bullwhip effect occurs when orders fluctuate more than the demand they are responding to. Those fluctuations can grow as orders move from a manufacturer to its suppliers. This experiment asked whether language models could limit that amplification while keeping stockouts low.

I tested gpt-4.1-mini and o4-mini through Azure, and phi4:14b and GPT OSS 120B locally through Ollama. Each model received either numerical inventory information alone or the same numbers with a tier-specific role and calendar context. This produced eight model-and-information combinations.

The data and method

The data was a 25-month synthetic demand series covering two Indian festive cycles. The fictional chain contained a vehicle manufacturer, a lighting supplier, and a component supplier, all serving one product with fixed one-month lead times. Every run began from the same initial conditions. The agents were stateless: each order was chosen without a memory of prior decisions.

Each language-model condition ran twenty times. Three deterministic policies ran once each: exponential smoothing with α = 0.30, passing demand through directly, and ordering up to a stock target with a fixed safety buffer. Their single runs reflected deterministic behaviour in this fixed environment, not comparable evidence of reliability under varying demand.

The primary measures were chain OVAR and stockouts, reported together. OVAR divides the variance of orders by the variance of demand at each tier, then averages across the three tiers. Values above one indicate amplification; below one indicate damping. The design required an OVAR difference of at least 0.5 for a practically meaningful claim. Lower variance alone was not enough if it came at the expense of service.

The model-condition means ranged from 4.33 to 6.35 OVAR. Every heuristic had both lower OVAR and fewer stockouts than every language-model configuration. This was not a case where the models traded extra inventory volatility for better service than the baselines. All seven stated hypotheses were rejected.

Context did not have a consistent effect. For gpt-4.1-mini, OVAR fell from 4.70 to 4.47, a reduction below the practical-importance threshold. For phi4:14b it rose from 4.33 to 6.35, with a standard deviation of 2.53 in the context condition. The extra information made that model's ordering much less predictable across runs. The reasoning models did not provide the expected operational advantage.

What the result means

The tested models were directly responsible for choosing order quantities. Their failure here does not show that language models cannot assist with other supply-chain work. Reading documents, interpreting unusual signals, and advising a planner are different tasks and were not evaluated.

Stateless decision-making is one possible contributor to the outcome, but this design did not independently vary memory. The result should therefore remain a comparison of these agents, prompts, baselines, and one synthetic series—not a universal explanation of model behaviour or a verdict on all replenishment systems.

Next steps

The architectural question was whether a model could inform an ordering formula rather than replace it. That became the next line of investigation. The baseline here supplies the reference point: any added model needs to improve an operational outcome, not merely produce a plausible explanation for an order.
