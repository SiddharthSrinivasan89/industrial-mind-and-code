Can small AI models diagnose machine faults?

Siddharth Srinivasan · June 2026

Running a small model locally is only the first step toward an industrial assistant. It also needs to answer correctly and produce output that software can use. I tested four small language models on fault-diagnosis questions, keeping those two requirements separate.

A local model is not yet a maintenance assistant

The models were Nemotron-3 Nano 4B, Gemma 3 4B, Phi-4-mini, and Phi-4-mini-reasoning. I wanted a baseline for local assistance from roughly four-billion-parameter models—not evidence that they could replace maintenance engineers.

All runs used Ollama on a workstation. This tests local inference and model footprint, not an edge device operating beside industrial machinery.

Testing answers and the ability to use them

IBM Research's FailureSensorIQ is a public multiple-choice benchmark about industrial sensors and failure modes. I used 2,667 single-answer questions across ten asset classes, including motors, pumps, compressors, and gas turbines. These are questions with answer options, not live sensor traces or maintenance interventions.

For each question, I recorded correctness and whether software could read the response in the required format. A usable answer can still be wrong. An unreadable answer creates a different problem: the application cannot use it. The study set a 5% unusable-output threshold for further consideration.

I ran the full question set sequentially to expose serving problems that a short sample might miss. Where clean comparisons were available, I varied temperature—the setting controlling sampling randomness—without changing model weights. Nemotron ran at temperature 0.6.

Nemotron had the best clean full-set result. Its 51.8% accuracy sits above the benchmark's published blind-guessing reference of approximately 27.5% and below the human-expert mean of 60.2%. Those are benchmark reference figures, not a new human comparison conducted here. The remaining error rate does not justify autonomous maintenance decisions.

Lowering Phi-4-mini's temperature improved observed accuracy and reduced unusable output enough to meet the study's gate. Gemma changed little across its two settings. Temperature mattered differently for these models; the results do not establish a general rule that lower is always better.

The missing result was also an integration warning

Phi-4-mini-reasoning has no clean full-set result and is excluded from the table. A separate 240-question, single-asset diagnostic at temperature 0.3 produced 40% unusable output. That partial, degraded run cannot serve as a fair model-ranking score.

Some questions that failed late in a long run answered cleanly after a fresh model load. That points toward serving-state degradation. Pressure in the runtime's cached working state is a proposed explanation, not a confirmed mechanism. Nemotron's architecture was not independently tested as an explanation for its endurance either.

What I would carry forward

The findings make Nemotron the primary candidate for further assistance work, with low-temperature Gemma as a non-reasoning alternative. Neither is approved for deployment by this study. Asset-specific validation, long-run serving checks, and grounding or human confirmation are still needed beyond the bare model.

Phi-4-mini-reasoning needs a clean full-set rerun before its reasoning performance can be compared fairly. This experiment establishes a baseline and exposes integration risks; it does not show that a deployed assistant improves maintenance outcomes. The hypotheses were not pre-registered.
