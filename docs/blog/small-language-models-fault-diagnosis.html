Can small AI models diagnose machine faults?

Siddharth Srinivasan · June 2026

A model that can run locally may be useful near industrial equipment—but fitting on the hardware is not the same as answering reliably. I tested four small language models on industrial fault-diagnosis questions, measuring correctness and usable output separately.

The models and the question

The models were Nemotron-3 Nano 4B, Gemma 3 4B, Phi-4-mini, and Phi-4-mini-reasoning. The question was whether these roughly four-billion-parameter models offered a useful baseline for local assistance, not whether they could replace maintenance engineers.

All runs used Ollama on a workstation. The study therefore provides local-inference and model-footprint evidence, not validation on an edge device installed beside machinery.

The data and method

IBM Research's FailureSensorIQ is a public multiple-choice benchmark about relationships between industrial sensors and failure modes. I used 2,667 single-answer questions across ten asset classes, including motors, pumps, compressors, and gas turbines. These are benchmark questions, not live sensor traces or maintenance interventions.

Each question has one correct option. I recorded both answer accuracy and whether the response could be read by software in the required format. A correctly formatted answer can still be wrong; an answer that the parser cannot use creates a separate integration failure. The study used a 5% unusable-output threshold for further consideration.

The full question set was run sequentially to expose serving problems that a short sample might miss. Where clean comparisons were available, I varied temperature—the setting that controls sampling randomness—while keeping the model weights fixed. Nemotron ran at temperature 0.6.

Nemotron had the best clean full-set result. The benchmark's published reference points are approximately 27.5% for blind guessing and 60.2% for the human-expert mean; those expert figures are benchmark references, not a new human comparison conducted here. A score of 51.8% leaves substantial room for error and does not justify autonomous maintenance decisions.

Lowering Phi-4-mini's temperature improved both its observed accuracy and its usable-output rate, bringing the latter inside the study's gate. Gemma's scores changed little across its two settings. These are model-specific observations, not a general rule that lower temperature improves every task.

The incomplete reasoning-model run

Phi-4-mini-reasoning produced no clean full-set result and is excluded from the table. A 240-question, single-asset diagnostic at temperature 0.3 had 40% unusable output. That partial, degraded run is not a valid model-ranking score.

Questions that failed late in a long run answered cleanly after the model was freshly loaded. This points toward serving-state degradation. Pressure in the runtime's cached working state is a proposed explanation, not a confirmed mechanism. Likewise, the idea that Nemotron's architecture explains its endurance was not isolated experimentally.

Next steps

The existing findings identify Nemotron as the primary candidate for further assistance work and low-temperature Gemma as a non-reasoning alternative. Neither choice is deployment approval. Follow-up work needs asset-specific validation, checks over long serving runs, and grounding or human confirmation above the bare model.

A clean full-set rerun of Phi-4-mini-reasoning remains necessary before comparing its reasoning performance fairly. The present study establishes a baseline and exposes integration risks; it does not show that a deployed assistant improves maintenance outcomes. The hypotheses were not pre-registered.
