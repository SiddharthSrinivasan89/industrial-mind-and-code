Better classifications, similar ordering outcomes

Siddharth Srinivasan · April 2026

Does better interpretation of a disruption lead to better orders? I tested four models that translated seasonal and event information into five possible labels. Context improved directional classification, but order variance stayed close across the tested conditions.

Interpreting signals without calculating the order

The models were gpt-4.1-mini, o4-mini, phi4:14b, and nemotron-super 120B. Each chose a direction label, from strong decrease to strong increase. Software mapped the label to one of five safety-stock multipliers between 0.80 and 1.30, and an Order-Up-To formula calculated the order.

This kept numerical execution in code and gave the model the interpretation task. The question was whether more accurate labels would improve supply-chain outcomes, not merely whether the labels were valid.

A simulated chain with disruptions

The fictional three-tier chain used 36 months of synthetic demand shaped around Indian automotive seasonality, with simulated pandemic, geopolitical-conflict, and port-strike events. Lead times and the proportion of orders fulfilled varied randomly. The event headlines were supplied by the simulation, not drawn from live news.

Each model received numbers alone, calendar and seasonal context, or context plus event text. The two frontier models ran twenty repetitions per condition; the local models ran ten. Because the environment itself was stochastic, baselines ran one hundred times.

I measured chain OVAR—the average across tiers of order variance divided by demand variance—alongside stockouts, directional accuracy, and label compliance. OVAR above one means amplification. These measures separate recognising a direction from controlling the consequences of an order.

Different classifications, a narrow range of ordering results

All twelve model-condition means fell between 1.726 and 1.780 OVAR. The full range was 0.054, smaller than the within-condition standard deviations of 0.088–0.133. The study called this clustering the “Equaliser Effect.” That describes the observed values; no formal equivalence test established that the conditions were interchangeable.

Phi4:14b with context had the lowest observed model mean, 1.726. The formula-only Order-Up-To baseline recorded 1.767, while exponential smoothing reached 1.185. Model results clustered near the formula-only baseline rather than approaching smoothing.

Smoothing did not lead on every measure. It averaged 89.62 stockout tier-periods per run, compared with 86.95 for Order-Up-To and 84.7–87.0 for the model conditions. Its lower order variance came with a less favourable stockout result.

What the extra information changed

Blind directional accuracy ranged from 0.41 to 0.48. Calendar context raised it to approximately 0.72–0.80, and event text brought some conditions as high as 0.84. Comparable gains did not appear in OVAR. For every model, the event-text condition had slightly higher OVAR than the calendar-context condition.

The hypotheses that AI would beat smoothing, that event text would reduce OVAR, and that it would improve disruption-period direction accuracy by at least ten percentage points were not supported. The compliance threshold passed, as did the hypothesis that world events materially changed OVAR.

Removing events from the matched phi4 conditions increased OVAR to about 2.08–2.12. That check matters because the variance ratio responds to the demand environment as well as the ordering policy; it is not a score for the model in isolation.

A separate instruction to default to neutral shifted mean OVAR by −0.015 to +0.005 across six matched cells. Here, neutral meant a multiplier of 1.0 followed by the ordering formula—not “place no order.” The label's operational meaning came from the code that used it.

The question this left open

The clustering is consistent with the lookup and downstream formula limiting what better classification could change. It does not prove that perfect classification could never help. The finding is bounded by stochastic supply conditions, one synthetic demand profile, and one lookup design.

The next experiment removed the language model and supplied perfect simulation-derived labels and deterministic rules instead. That tested the unresolved question directly. V4b provides the reason for that test, not its answer.

Conclusion

Better directional classification did not translate into comparable reductions in order variance. The model-condition means clustered near the formula-only baseline, while smoothing had lower variance but more stockouts. These observations motivated testing the control interface itself; they do not establish model equivalence or prove that better classification could never help.
