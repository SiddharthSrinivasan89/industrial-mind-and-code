Correctly formatted labels, wider order swings

Siddharth Srinivasan · April 2026

If a language model struggles to choose a numerical control setting, would a multiple-choice label work better? In this experiment, every response used a valid label—but the resulting orders still fluctuated much more than those from a simple smoothing rule.

The question and the model

I tested gpt-4.1-mini as a classifier inside a replenishment system. At each month and supply-chain tier it chose one of five labels, from strong decrease through neutral to strong increase. A fixed lookup converted that label to a buffer multiplier, and an Order-Up-To formula calculated the actual order.

The model never supplied an order quantity or a numerical multiplier. This separated output-format reliability from the question that matters operationally: did the resulting policy control inventory and order variability effectively?

The data and method

The fictional system contained a vehicle manufacturer, an ancillary supplier, and a component maker. It used one product, fixed one-month lead times, and 25 months of synthetic demand representing Indian automotive seasonality. There were no injected disruptions or demand shocks.

The model received numbers alone, numbers with calendar context, or a stateful version with recent history. Each condition ran twenty times. Exponential smoothing and a fixed-buffer version of the same ordering formula supplied deterministic baselines.

I measured chain OVAR—the average across tiers of order variance divided by demand variance—alongside stockouts and label compliance. Values below one mean order variability is damped; above one mean it is amplified. Compliance measures whether a response is in the allowed format, not whether it is the right operational choice.

Label compliance was 1.000 throughout, with no fallback responses. The interface worked as intended. Nevertheless, the model conditions produced roughly six to seven times the OVAR of exponential smoothing, which also had fewer stockouts.

Calendar context lowered OVAR relative to the numbers-only condition, but it did not close the gap to either baseline. The discrete interface constrained what the model could output without making those outputs effective. In this setting, valid structured output and good control were clearly different achievements.

The experiment initially used ten repetitions per condition, then expanded to twenty. The reported means changed by less than one standard deviation on the headline measures. The twenty-run set is the result of record; that stability check does not establish generalisation to other demand profiles.

Limits and next steps

Only one model, one synthetic demand series, and one simplified supply chain were tested. The lookup values were chosen from domain logic and were not established as optimal. The result therefore applies to this label-to-multiplier interface, not to classification-based control in general.

This was the intermediate V4a variant. The next experiment, V4b, carried the label architecture into a setting with disruptions and stochastic supply conditions. That continuation tests a different environment; its results should not be merged with the numbers here.
