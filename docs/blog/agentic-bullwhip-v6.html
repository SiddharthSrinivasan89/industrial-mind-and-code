Adaptive smoothing in supply chain replenishment

Siddharth Srinivasan · April 2026

Can AI help a supply chain by adjusting a forecast rather than deciding how much to order? In this simulation, AI selected how quickly the forecast responded to demand, while a formula calculated the orders. The tested conditions dampened order variability on average—but the results did not establish that AI improved on a fixed smoothing setting.

A smaller decision for AI

I tested gpt-4.1-mini, o4-mini, and GPT OSS 120B in a deliberately bounded role. At each period and supply-chain tier, the model chose a smoothing coefficient, α, from 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, or 0.7. A larger value gives the latest demand observation more influence over the forecast; a smaller value makes the forecast less responsive to that observation.

The ordering formula then used the smoothed forecast to calculate the order. There was no safety-stock multiplier and no Order-Up-To calculation. The question was whether letting AI adjust this one parameter could dampen order variability and remain competitive with the same formula using a fixed coefficient.

How I tested it

The experiment used a fictional three-tier supply chain, one product, and a 25-month synthetic demand series shaped around Indian automotive seasonality. Lead times were fixed at one month, with no stochastic fill rates or injected disruption events. This was a simpler environment than the earlier V4 and V5 experiments, so any improvement across those experiments cannot be attributed to the ordering architecture alone.

Each model ran ten repetitions under each of three conditions. The first supplied numerical state alone. The second added calendar and seasonal context. The third was a stateful version with recent α history. Fixed-coefficient baselines ran once because the environment was deterministic.

I measured chain OVAR: each tier's order variance divided by demand variance, then averaged across the chain. A value below one means that orders vary less than demand on this measure. I also tracked stockouts, because smoother orders would be less useful if they came with worse availability.

All nine adaptive condition means were below one, ranging from 0.535 to 0.741. GPT OSS 120B with numerical state alone had the lowest observed OVAR, at 0.535, and averaged 4.4 stockouts. The fixed α = 0.3 baseline achieved about 0.545 with five stockouts.

That comparison is central to the result. The AI condition had the lower point estimate, but the 0.010 gap was small relative to its run-to-run variation. These results do not establish that adding AI beats fixed smoothing.

More context did not produce steadier orders

Adding calendar and seasonal context increased OVAR for all three models. The history conditions fell between their numbers-only and context counterparts. In this setup, additional context came with more variable orders on the measured outcome.

Why that happened remains unresolved. The averages do not support the simple explanation that context made the models choose a higher α: mean α was actually lower with context than with numbers alone. Establishing a mechanism would require separate evidence about the timing and distribution of those choices.

What the follow-up clarified—and what it did not

Four follow-up conditions ran five repetitions each. They removed seasonal guidance and instructed the models to use numerical evidence. The computed-signal variants also replaced calendar input with derived statistics. All four α options remained available.

The published five-run GPT OSS 120B computed-signal batch reached 0.585, compared with 0.739 in the original context condition. Another stored five-run batch reached 0.573. The available records do not explain why the published account selected one batch, so I report both without pooling them or choosing a new headline result.

The revised gpt-4.1-mini instruction condition reached 0.596, while its computed-signal condition reached 0.679. The latter remained closer to the original context result than to the numbers-only result. The follow-up therefore did not uniformly meet its stated closeness criterion.

The five runs of that computed-signal condition also repeated the same reported α distributions. They should not be read as five diverse demand scenarios. Together, these observations do not establish a general mechanism for removing the context penalty.

What I take from this—and what comes next

Giving AI a small parameter-setting role produced damping condition means in this deterministic simulation. That is a useful result, but it is not evidence that a language model is necessary. Fixed smoothing already dampened variance and remained competitive without the inference cost or integration complexity.

The next question is whether the small observed point-estimate advantage persists with more repetitions and a broader range of demand or disruption settings. Those extensions are not results of this study. The findings remain limited to three models, a four-value option set, one synthetic demand series, and the stated run counts. The hypotheses were not externally pre-registered.

Conclusion

Adaptive parameter selection produced variance-damping condition means in this simulation, but did not establish an advantage over fixed smoothing. The fixed baseline remained competitive without adding a language model. The context penalty and mixed follow-up results leave its mechanism unresolved; neither a general debiasing method nor an architecture-only explanation for improvements across experiments has been established.
