The Architecture That Finally Worked

Five versions of failing taught me what the wrong lever looks like. V6 changes the lever. The AI now controls the smoothing parameter α inside exponential smoothing itself. Every AI condition produced OVAR below 1.0. The best result marginally beats the fixed α=0.3 baseline. First positive result in this research program.

Date April 2026
Experiment Agentic Bullwhip Effect Version 6 — StatelessSwing Adaptive α
Design 3 models · 3 conditions + V6b debiased · 10 runs each · 25-month simulation
Author Siddharth Srinivasan · industrialmindandcode.ai

TL;DR

V6 gives the AI control of the exponential smoothing parameter α rather than a safety stock multiplier. It selects from {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7} per period per tier. Every AI condition produced OVAR below 1.0 — the first time in this program that any AI configuration dampened variance rather than amplified it.

Key findings

What I found

For the first time in this program, every AI condition produced OVAR below 1.0. In V1 through V5, AI configurations amplified order variance by 30% to 1,200%. In V6, the worst result is 0.739 — still damping.

  1. The best AI condition matches the fixed baseline. gpt-oss 120B in blind conditions reached OVAR 0.535 with 4.4 stockouts. The fixed α=0.3 baseline is 0.545 with 5 stockouts. The difference is within the confidence interval.
  2. Context increases α selections and adds variance. Models given seasonal information consistently choose higher α values. The debiased conditions close most of the gap: oss120b_ctx_computed reaches OVAR 0.585 against oss120b_context at 0.739.
  3. The 120B model defaults to α=0.3 in blind conditions — the standard exponential smoothing value. Smaller models select α=0.7 more often, adding variance. The advantage disappears when context is added.

Discussion

Why this architecture is different

In V3b through V5, the AI controlled a safety stock multiplier — the buffer held above the forecast. Adjusting the buffer changes inventory levels but does not directly affect how volatile orders are. α determines how much each demand observation moves the forecast; lower values produce stable orders, higher values produce reactive ones.

When models receive seasonal context, they select higher α values. The reasoning is sound; the calibration is off. Restricting the option set or providing explicit correction recovers most of the blind condition’s advantage, as the V6b results show.

Methodology note

All scenarios, companies, and products are fictional. The demand series is synthetic, calibrated to Indian automotive seasonal patterns across 25 months, using deterministic lead times to isolate the architecture change from stochastic supply-side effects tested in V4/V5. Results apply to a 3-tier exponential smoothing architecture where the AI selects α ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7} per period; do not generalise beyond this scope.
