What happens when you place a probabilistic system inside a deterministic environment? I run controlled experiments that put LLM agents into industrial engineering problems — supply chains, maintenance systems, production planning — and measure their performance against analytical baselines and share the findings.

About

Why this work exists

I love machines and I love technology. I was always fascinated by these two worlds.

Armed with a Bachelor's in Industrial Engineering and deep technical expertise by working for a digital engineering software company, I sit in the middle of how AI infrastructure gets built, sold, and deployed at enterprise scale.

There is no shortage of people writing about AI. Most of it in a general point of view. Very little of it is specific to the domains where I have actual experience: manufacturing, supply chain, maintenance, production planning and core industrial engineering concepts.

I started this research program because I wanted to understand AI in the domain I know well, through my own analysis, rather than relying on what others claim. Not to prove that AI works. Not to prove that it doesn't. To find out where it holds up when you test it properly and identify domains in Industrial Engineering and beyond where it can add true value.

Each experiment takes a foundational IE problem, builds a simulation, places LLM agents in the decision-making role, and compares the result against what a formula or a heuristic would have done. Two experiments are published. A third is in progress. The results are below.

Agentic Bullwhip Effect: Version 2 Published

SUPPLY CHAIN · HEURISTICS VS LLMs · 11,520 LLM CALLS

Version 2 asked the question that Version 1 left open: do any of these LLM configurations actually outperform a simple heuristic? I tested four models — frontier and local, lightweight and reasoning — against three deterministic baselines across 20 runs per condition. 11,520 LLM calls total.

  • Every heuristic outperformed every LLM on both order variance (OVAR) and stockout count simultaneously. Exponential smoothing — a formula from the 1950s — achieved OVAR 0.54. The best LLM achieved 4.33. Not a close result.
  • Adding business context to the local model (phi4:14b) made its behaviour unstable rather than better, with run-to-run variance so high that some runs looked reasonable and others were extreme.
  • The reasoning-tier models, despite generating over a million reasoning tokens, showed no measurable improvement in ordering performance over the lightweight tier.

Agentic Bullwhip Effect: Version 1 Published

SUPPLY CHAIN · 2x2 FACTORIAL · GPT-4.1-MINI vs O1

Version 1 was the starting point — a 2×2 factorial design crossing model tier against context treatment, five runs per condition, to establish a baseline for how LLM agents handle ordering decisions in a three-tier supply chain.

  • All four configurations amplified demand variability. What I did not expect was the degree of variation across configurations, and one result in particular.
  • The context × reasoning condition (o1 with full business context) produced an inverted tier pattern — the OEM tier was the noisiest, the component tier was the quietest. I have not found this pattern described in the bullwhip literature I have reviewed.
  • I had set up the analytical baseline specifically to have something to check against. The baseline did its job — it made the anomaly visible. Version 2 was designed in part to see whether this pattern replicated at a higher sample size.

Methodology

How experiments are designed

01

Analytical baselines, not model comparisons

Each experiment measures LLM performance against a deterministic analytical method that already exists for the problem — exponential smoothing, order-up-to policies, SPC rules. The question is not which model is better than which. It is whether any model outperforms the established approach.

02

Controlled simulation environments

Parameters are synthetic but grounded in published literature. All scenarios are fictional. No proprietary data is used.

03

Statistical design

Factorial designs with 20–100 replications per cell. Hypotheses are pre-registered. Effect size thresholds are set before the experiment runs. If an effect does not clear the threshold, it is not treated as meaningful, regardless of statistical significance.
